SSO for Smaller SaaS Teams: When and How to Build It

Published 2026-04-30 · 6 min read

SSO often gets treated as a "someday" feature — something to build once a large enough enterprise customer asks for it. That's a reasonable default early on, but it's worth understanding what SSO actually involves before that request lands as a deal-blocking requirement with a tight deadline attached.

What SSO actually means for a SaaS product

At its core, SSO lets a customer's employees log into your product using their own company's identity provider — Okta, Azure AD/Entra, Google Workspace, and similar — instead of a separate username and password just for your app. For enterprise buyers, this isn't a nice-to-have; it's often a hard requirement tied to their own security and access policies, since it lets their IT team control who has access to your product without managing separate credentials.

SAML vs. OIDC

The two common protocols are SAML and OIDC (built on OAuth 2.0). OIDC is generally simpler to implement and is well supported by modern identity providers, while SAML remains common in larger, more traditional enterprise environments. Many SSO implementations end up supporting both, since you don't always get to choose which protocol a given customer's identity provider uses.

Where teams underestimate the scope

The login flow itself is usually the easy part. The parts that take real design work are: mapping an incoming SSO identity to an existing account (or deciding when to create a new one), handling users who need to belong to multiple organizations, deciding what happens when a company's IT team deprovisions a user on their end, and making sure your existing authentication code path doesn't quietly break for non-SSO customers while you build this in.

A practical way to scope it

Rather than building a fully generic "support any identity provider" system on day one, it's usually more practical to implement support for OIDC first (covers the majority of modern identity providers), validate it end-to-end with one real customer, and expand to SAML or additional edge cases as actual demand shows up. This keeps the initial build scoped and testable, instead of speculative.

Security details that are easy to miss

A few things worth being deliberate about: verifying tokens and assertions properly rather than trusting whatever the identity provider sends, deciding how session length and re-authentication should work for SSO users versus regular users, and making sure admin actions (like changing a user's role) can't be bypassed just because someone authenticated via SSO. These aren't exotic edge cases — they're the actual substance of "SSO done properly" versus "SSO that technically works."

The takeaway

SSO is very buildable for a smaller SaaS team, but it's more than a login screen swap — it touches account mapping, session handling, and admin permissions. Scoping it deliberately (OIDC first, validated with a real customer, expanded from there) keeps it from turning into an open-ended project when a deal deadline is on the line.
